Class exercises in hydraulician: what works today, what needs changes, what it cannot do

Every topic on the 3rd/4th-year syllabus, mapped against what the solver, the scenes and the instruments actually deliver. Verdicts are grounded in the code as it stands and in the measured validation numbers already in the project notes.

The instrument rack you already have

The pedagogical trick available here and in no physical lab: every student runs their own flume, and the class pools one data point each into a curve. The kit, per student, in the browser:

Five hard limits — the triage rule

Almost every verdict below follows from one of these. If a proposed exercise trips none of them, it very likely works today.

  1. It is a vertical slice, one metre wide. Nothing lateral or three-dimensional exists: no V-notches, no venturi flumes (width contractions), no circular pipe sections, no compound-channel floodplains, no wave refraction. Discharges are unit discharges (m²/s); "areas" are gap heights. The plan-view mode is a different slice, not a third dimension.
  2. Gauge pressure cannot go below zero in free-surface mode — the EOS is p = c²·max(f−1, 0). Where real water would go sub-atmospheric, this water separates at 0 gauge. So: no true siphons, no suction lift, no NPSH; column separation happens, but at the wrong absolute pressure.
  3. Air is a passive vacuum. Voids have no pressure of their own, so nothing pneumatic works: no air vessels, no trapped-air springs, no measurable aeration demand; nappes are always perfectly ventilated.
  4. No rigid or rotating bodies. The valve and the wave piston are the only movers. Pumps, turbines, floats and rotameters have no representation; the spout (a momentum source) and the head-driven reservoir are the only "machines".
  5. Resistance is delivered by the grid, not dialled in. The effective friction is Cf + wall function + eddy viscosity + bed rasterisation, and it depends on cells-per-depth (delivered n ≈ 0.07 at ~8 cells, ≈ 0.02 at ~25). Everything ∝ V² works beautifully; absolute Moody/Colebrook coefficients do not. Corollary for every pooled exercise: pin the whole class to the same Resolution setting.

Two smaller ones worth knowing: a wave must be ≥ ~2 cells tall to survive the interface (so plunging breakers are out of reach — breaks are spilling); and drawn sandbox geometry currently has no save/share, so student-built rigs travel as recipe cards, not files.

Exercise formats used below

POOLED SWEEPTemplate rig + one parameter per student + a gauge. Each reports one steady number to a shared sheet; the instructor's live plot IS the equation. The workhorse.
PREDICT → RUNStudents commit a computed prediction (on paper, before touching the app), then run and score |error|. Turns every formula into a wager.
MYSTERY COEFFICIENTInstructor hands out a rig with a hidden setting (roughness, gap, crest height). Teams get three runs and their instruments to identify it. Model calibration in miniature.
DESIGN TO SPECMeet a target — surge below X m, jump inside the box, no surcharge at 2×DWF — with the cheapest geometry. Verified live in front of everyone.
JIGSAWEach group owns one regime segment (a q, a period, a scale); the assembled master chart only makes sense when everyone's piece lands.
BAKE-OFFMeasure the same quantity several independent ways; reconcile and budget the uncertainty.
BREAK-IT CLINICDeliberately mis-set boundary conditions (tailwater below yc, inlet pinned under the backwater); students diagnose from gauges. The project's own hard-won pitfalls, taught forwards.

Part A — Fluids in pipes

A1 · Velocity & discharge measurement

READY AS-IS

The venturi and orifice scenes exist and are validated (throat jet 19.4 vs 20.3 m/s ideal; efflux Cv ≈ 0.97). Everything else is a sandbox build. All "areas" become gap widths per metre.

Concept / equationHow / why not
Continuity Q = AV (here q = hV)✓Column q printed in the hover readout; conservation itself is machine-precision by construction
Venturi meter Q = C_d·a₂·√(2gΔh / (1 − (a₂/a₁)²))✓venturi scene or drawn contraction; two gauges are the pressure taps
Pitot / stagnation V = √(2gΔh)✓Probe the stagnation point on any wall facing a jet; compare against static head beside it
Orifice: V = C_v√(2gh), C_d = C_c·C_v✓jet scene; overflow lip holds h steady; Cv ≈ 0.97 measured
Vena contracta, Cc✓Visible at the lip. Bonus: 2D free-streamline theory gives Cc = π/(π+2) = 0.611 for a sharp slot — the model is exactly the 2D case the theory solves
Borda mouthpiece Cc = ½✓Draw a short re-entrant lip on the jet tank (the scene's own tips suggest it)
Velocity–area gauging (current-meter traverse)✓Rake gives u(z); students integrate by mid-section rule and check against the column q
Float / dye timing✓*Time a dye-line between stations for mean V; concentration-based dilution gauging is out (dye is a visual tracer)

Exercise · pooled sweep — "the class draws the rating"

Setup: venturi scene, gauges on the upstream barrel and the throat. Vary: each student a different reservoir level (or drawn throat gap). Report: steady q and Δh. Plot: q against √Δh — a straight line whose slope is the meter coefficient; back out Cd and argue about why it is below 0.98 (2D, resolution, real losses — exactly why meters are calibrated).

Exercise · jigsaw — "three orifices, three theories"

Thirds of the class build sharp-edged, rounded (bellmouth), and Borda re-entrant openings on the jet tank. Measure jet width / opening ratio and efflux speed; the three clusters should land near 0.61, ~1.0, and 0.50 — one physical law, three classical coefficients, and the Borda value provable in ten lines of momentum on the board.

Exercise · bake-off — "four meters, one flow"

One channel, fixed q. Teams measure the discharge four ways: venturi Δh, weir head (Part F1), rake integration, dye-line timing. Reconcile, rank by uncertainty, discuss which instrument they would install and why.

A2 · The momentum equation

READY AS-IS

Everything measurable that a control-volume analysis needs (depths, velocities, pressures, q) is instrumented, and there is a momentum-flux display mode. What does not exist is a force transducer — forces are computed from the CV, never read off a dial. Pedagogically that is arguably the point of the momentum equation; a wall-pressure integrator would close the loop and is a modest change.

Concept / equationHow / why not
ΣF = ρQ(β₂V₂ − β₁V₁) + pressure terms✓All right-hand-side quantities measurable; β from the rake (see F4)
Jet on a plate F = ρqV(1 − cos θ) per m✓*Build with spout + wall; verify via stagnation head = V²/2g at the plate (probe), not via a force dial
Force on a sluice gate✓F/ρ = ½g(y₁²−y₂²) − q(V₂−V₁) from measured y₁, y₂, q — s3 scene or sandbox
Hydraulic jump = momentum function equality, M = q²/(gy) + y²/2✓The jump box measures both conjugates; M₁ ≈ M₂ while E₁ ≠ E₂ is the whole lesson
Force on bends / anchor thrust (pressurised)✓*Compute from probed p and V at the two legs of a drawn bend; no reaction readout
Direct force-on-structure readoutΔIntegrate p over solid-adjacent cells inside a user box — a contained overlay/readback feature
Exercise · pooled sweep — "gate thrust curve"

Setup: sluice gate rig (s3 or drawn). Vary: gate opening a across the class. Report: y₁, y₂, q. Plot: computed thrust F against a. Then spring the trap: compare with the hydrostatic estimate ½ρg(y₁−a)² and let the discrepancy introduce why momentum, not statics, sizes a gate.

Exercise · predict → run — "momentum audit of a jump"

On h23, each student computes M and E on both sides from the jump box's own y₁, y₂, Fr₁. Momentum should balance within a few percent; energy should not, by exactly ΔE = (y₂−y₁)³/(4y₁y₂), which the box also prints. One overlay of ten students' audits makes "jumps conserve momentum and destroy energy" an observed fact.

A3 · Energy and flow of water in pipes

READY AS-IS

Bernoulli exchange is validated in the venturi scene; four gauges make a live HGL. The two honest exclusions: nothing sub-atmospheric (limit 2 — no siphons), and no absolute Moody/Colebrook friction factors (limit 5 — hf ∝ V² is clean, the constant is the grid's).

Concept / equationHow / why not
Bernoulli with losses; EGL & HGL✓Gauges = piezometers along a duct; hover at the throat shows p/ρg falling as V²/2g rises
Darcy–Weisbach h_f = f·(L/D)·V²/2g✓*hf ∝ V² demonstrable cleanly; f is the delivered value, not Colebrook's — frame as "measure this pipe's f"
Moody chart / Colebrook–White; laminar f = 64/Re✗Limit 5; solver is high-Re by construction (Smagorinsky closure)
Sudden expansion ΔH = (V₁−V₂)²/2g (Borda–Carnot)✓Draw the step; gauges either side; separation visible in dye/vorticity
Diffuser vs contraction (why recovery is imperfect)✓The venturi scene's own headline — "the gap is the loss"
Entry length / developing flow✓Dye timelines shear into the profile; rakes at successive stations
Siphons✗Limit 2 — the crest separates at 0 gauge. (Ten minutes of "why did it break?" is a genuinely good lesson on atmospheric pressure.)
Cavitation threshold✓*Venturi tip: raise the reservoir until throat head hits zero — right behaviour, wrong absolute pressure (0 gauge, not −10 m)

Exercise · pooled sweep — "the friction law without the chart"

Setup: one long straight duct rig from a head-driven reservoir. Vary: reservoir level per student. Report: steady V (rake) and head drop over a marked 10 m (two gauges). Plot: log hf vs log V — the class gets slope ≈ 2 out of their own points, and the intercept is the delivered f for this pipe. Follow-up discussion: why the intercept is not in any textbook table, and why real networks are calibrated.

Exercise · mystery coefficient — "find the throttle"

Instructor secretly draws a partial obstruction somewhere in a long covered pipe (zoomed-out view hides it). Teams get 4 gauges and must locate the loss and estimate its K from the HGL kink alone — the exact skill of finding a half-shut valve in a real main.

Part B — Pumps, networks & distribution

B1 · Pipe network analysis

READY AS-IS

Scoped verdict: everything that fits in a vertical plane works now — pipes in series and parallel, and the classic three-reservoir junction. Multi-loop grids don't fit the geometry (and are EPANET's job anyway); the sim's contribution is making the loop law physically believable before Hardy Cross turns it into bookkeeping.

Concept / equationHow / why not
Node continuity ΣQ = 0✓*Measure branch flows with a rake across each branch (× gap). Caveat: the per-column q sums vertically stacked pipes — separate branches in x, or use rakes
Loop law Σhf = 0; h = rQⁿ✓Two parallel ducts of different gap between two tanks = the minimal loop; gauges give each branch's hf, which must agree
Three-reservoir junction problem✓Three tanks at different levels piped to one junction — draws naturally in the vertical plane; junction head from a gauge
Hardy Cross iteration ΔQ = −Σh / (n·Σ|h/Q|)✓*Paper method, seeded with r-values the students measured — a good hybrid
Multi-loop grids, equivalent-pipe reductions at scale✗Topology needs crossings/plan geometry (limit 1); use EPANET and say so
Exercise · predict → run — "the parallel split"

Setup: template rig, two parallel ducts (gaps b and 2b) joining the same two tanks. Predict: the flow split from h = rQ² with r estimated from the A3 friction exercise. Run: rake both branches. The equal-head-loss constraint — the thing students never quite believe — is sitting right there on two gauges reading the same number.

Exercise · pooled sweep — "when does reservoir B flip?"

Three-reservoir rig; A and C fixed, each student a different level for middle reservoir B. Report the three branch flows and the junction head. The pooled plot of QB against zB crosses zero at the junction head — the moment a supplier becomes a customer, found empirically by the class before anyone solves it analytically.

B2 · Pump–pipe systems

SOME CHANGES

Half of the operating-point diagram is measurable today: the system curve, because the head-driven reservoir is a constant-head source and the spout is a constant-flow source. The other half — a live H(Q) pump characteristic — needs a new boundary type. Note: a pressure-feedback inlet was tried once and reverted (it hunted), so this change is real work, not a tweak.

Concept / equationHow / why not
System curve H = H_static + kQ²✓Impose Q (spout into a rising main), gauge the delivery head; or impose H (reservoir) and read Q
Pump curve H = H₀ − aQ², liveΔNeeds a "pump band" boundary applying H(Q) with relaxation — the historically explosive part of the code, budget accordingly
Operating point (intersection)✓*Hybrid now: intersect a paper pump curve with the class-measured system curve, then verify by imposing the predicted Q and checking the head
Pumps in series / parallel✓*Paper transformation of the pump curve against the measured system curve; live only after the Δ above
NPSH, suction lift, cavitation at the impeller✗Limit 2 — water cannot be pulled into a suction line at all; there is no sub-atmospheric driving force
Specific speed, efficiency, motor sizing✗No machine (limit 4)
Exercise · pooled sweep — "measure the system curve, then commit to a duty"

Setup: rising main from a sump to an elevated tank, spout as the pump. Vary: spout velocity (= Q) per student. Report: steady delivery head from a gauge at the pump flange. Plot: H vs Q — fit Hs + kQ². Then hand out a manufacturer-style pump curve on paper, have everyone find the operating point graphically, and nominate one student to run that exact Q live: the gauge should land on the predicted head in front of the whole class.

NOT FEASIBLE

Adding machinery is not a "some changes" item — it is a different project. But the 4A15 equation list shows the topic's penstock half (H22–H26) is pure pipe hydraulics, and that is fully demonstrable — so a hydropower hour is salvageable after all:

Part C — Unsteady flow

C1 · Water hammer & surge protection

READY AS-IS

The strongest topic in the set, and the one no undergraduate lab can match: the hammer scene is validated (peak 39.0 m vs Joukowsky 41.1 m, −5%; period 3.0 s vs 4L/c = 2.8 s), and the wave speed itself is a slider (8–400 m/s, with c/g printed beside it). Students can do the impossible experiment: change the celerity of water.

Concept / equationHow / why not
Joukowsky ΔH = c·Δv / g✓Gauge on the pipe, slam with V; verified −5%
Reflection period T = 4L/c; the x–t diagram✓The gauge trace is a square wave; reflections legible; halve c and T doubles
Celerity vs stiffness c = √(K/ρ) / √(1 + KD/eE)✓*The formula stays on paper, but the c slider is its physical meaning made adjustable (slot width ↔ pipe elasticity)
Slow closure: Michaud ΔH = 2LV/(gT_c), Tc vs 2L/cΔThe valve is a binary slam. A fractional/scheduled valve (u_valve is already a float uniform) is the single highest-value small change in this list
Column separation on downsurge✓*Push c past ~90 in the hammer scene (its own tip); occurs at 0 gauge rather than vapour pressure (limit 2)
Surge tank mass oscillation T = 2π√(L·A_s / (g·A_p))✓Draw an open standpipe on the hammer pipeline; gauge the tank; per-metre widths replace areas
Air vessels / air valves✗Limit 3 — a trapped pocket has no pressure, so no spring
Friction damping of the surge✓Bulk damping slider to zero: "surges ring forever" — then bring it back
Exercise · pooled sweep — "the class discovers c"

Setup: hammer scene, gauge mid-pipe. Vary: each student throttles the nozzle differently (redraw the plate gap) → different steady v₀, probed before closure. Report: (v₀, ΔH) after slamming V. Plot: ΔH vs Δv — a straight line through the origin whose slope, ×g, is the celerity. The class measures c ≈ 70 m/s without ever being told it; then reveal the slider.

Exercise · pooled sweep — "T = 4L/c with your own valve"

Each student draws their own valve at a different x along the pipe and slams it: report (L, T) read from the gauge's square wave. The pooled line through the origin has slope 4/c. Doubles as a lesson in reading transient records.

Exercise · design to spec — "cheapest surge tank"

Spec: after valve closure, peak head at the valve must stay below a stated ceiling. Teams add an open standpipe — position and width are their design freedoms; narrowest total tank width wins. Verify live; then compare each design's oscillation period against 2π√(L·b_s/(g·b_p)) with the widths they drew.

Part D — Dimensional analysis & similarity

D1 · Dimensional analysis & similarity

READY AS-IS

The sandbox is a model hall: the same rig can be drawn at 1:1, 1:2 and 1:4 inside the 9 × 5 m domain. Froude similarity holds because gravity dominates; Reynolds and Weber similarity fail for exactly the reasons they fail in real model studies — which is the lecture.

Concept / equationHow / why not
Buckingham π; data collapse✓Pool the class's weir or gate data across scales and non-dimensionalise — the collapse is the theorem
Froude scaling: V ∝ √λ, q ∝ λ^1.5, T ∝ √λ, Q_model/Q_proto = λ^2.5 (3D) / λ^1.5 per m✓Geometric copies in the sandbox with q scaled accordingly
Reynolds similarity; scale effects✗ → ✓Unachievable — deliberately: the small model is visibly over-damped, which is the scale-effect lesson taught by demonstration
Weber number / surface tension✗No capillarity at all — name it as a missing physics, another scale-effect talking point
Grid convergence as "model scale" (numerics module bonus)✓Same scene at Low → Ultra Resolution: delivered Manning n falls from ~0.07 to ~0.02 as cells-per-depth grows — a live grid-refinement study
Exercise · jigsaw — "the scale ladder"

Thirds of the class build the same weir-and-approach rig at λ = 1, ½, ¼ (all dimensions, with q scaled by λ1.5). Each pools (H, q) points. Plotted raw: three separate curves. Plotted as Cd = q / (√g·H1.5) against H/P: one curve — mostly. The systematic droop of the smallest model at low heads is a genuine scale effect the students found themselves.

Part E — Linear wave theory

E1 · Linear wave theory

READY AS-IS

Four flume scenes span h/L = 0.59 to 0.05, and the orbital physics is validated: near-bed horizontal amplitude 0.37 of surface against 0.44 from cosh k(z+h)/sinh kh; vertical motion dying 244× in the deep flume. Waves shorter than ~2 cells in height do not survive the interface — instructions must keep waves long or tall, and plunging breakers are out.

Concept / equationHow / why not
Dispersion σ² = gk·tanh(kh); deep L = gT²/2π; shallow c = √(gh)✓Period is a slider (0.4–6 s); measure L by pausing and using the scale bar, or two gauges' phase lag
Orbital velocities and their decay with depth✓Orbit-tracer trails are the textbook figure, live; deep vs shallow flumes are the two limiting panels
Pressure attenuation p⁺ = ρg(H/2)·cosh k(z+h)/cosh kh✓A submerged gauge under waves is a pressure wave-recorder; recover H from depth and check against a near-surface gauge
Shoaling; breaking index H ≈ 0.78h; Iribarren ξ bands✓*Spilling (ξ ≈ 0.4) and surging (ξ ≈ 8) scenes exist and behave; plunging is unresolvable (interface ~2 cells)
Reflection, standing waves, seiche✓Steep beach reflects (its tip explains the choppy tank); nodes/antinodes measurable with two gauges; the flume seiche is real and findable
Group velocity cg = c/2 (deep)✓*Hand-toggle the piston for ~5 strokes and watch the envelope lag the crests — qualitative only
Refraction, diffraction✗Plan-view phenomena (limit 1)
Stokes drift✓The tracer loops deliberately fail to close; the near-bed return current is visible and explained in-scene
Exercise · pooled sweep — "dispersion, one period each"

Setup: deep-water flume. Vary: paddle period per student across 0.6–1.6 s. Report: (T, L) — pause and read L off the scale bar over several crests. Plot: L against T²: slope g/2π. Repeat three points in the shallow flume, where L = T√(gh) instead — the two regimes on one chart is the whole of linear wave theory's plumbing.

Exercise · bake-off — "the buried wave gauge"

Two gauges on one wave train: one just under the surface, one at the bed. Recover the surface wave height from the bed gauge alone using the cosh correction, and check against the direct measurement. Then move to the deep flume and watch the bed gauge go blind — precisely why real pressure recorders have a depth limit.

Exercise · jigsaw — "the Iribarren map"

Each pair takes one (stroke, period, beach) cell across both beach scenes, reports breaker behaviour (spills / surges / dies before breaking) and surf-zone width. Assembled on a ξ = tanβ/√(H₀/L₀) axis, the class's own observations reproduce the breaker-type banding — with an honest gap where plunging should be, and the reason why.

Part F — Open channel flow

F1 · Weirs, notches and flumes

READY AS-IS

Everything rectangular works; anything that meters by narrowing the width (V-notch, venturi/Parshall flume) is 3D and out (limit 1). The honest substitute teaches the same principle: a bed hump forcing critical depth is a flume's control mechanism in the vertical plane.

Concept / equationHow / why not
Rectangular sharp-crested q = ⅔·C_d·√(2g)·H^1.5✓Thin drawn wall; nappe always ventilated (limit 3 — say so)
Broad-crested q = 1.705·C_d·H^1.5; critical control on the crest✓m1/c13 weirs or drawn; Froude view shows the critical transition sitting on the crest
V-notch Q = (8/15)C_d√(2g)·tan(θ/2)·H^2.5✗Inherently 3D
Venturi / Parshall flumes✗Width contraction — substitute the hump and discuss the equivalence
Modular limit, drowned-flow reduction✓Tailwater slider drowns the weir on demand
Ogee/spillway profiles, Cd vs H/Hd✓*Drawable but rasterised chunky — qualitative shapes only
Exercise · pooled sweep — "one point each, the exponent appears"

Setup: template broad-crested weir, gauge upstream for H over crest. Vary: inflow q per student (with the standing rule: keep any tailwater ≥ 1.3 yc). Report: (q, H). Plot: log q vs log H — the class-measured slope should land near 1.5, and the intercept gives Cd. This is the purest version of the format: one run each, ten minutes, and a rating curve nobody was given.

Exercise · pooled sweep — "drowning the weir"

Fix q; each student a different tailwater level. Report the upstream head and whether the control still holds. The pooled curve of q/qfree (or upstream level) against submergence ratio finds the modular limit empirically — the number design guides quote as ~0.66–0.8 depending on the weir.

In the sandbox: produce and screenshot as many distinct, correctly-labelled profile classes as possible in one build (score sheet; overlay chips as evidence). A2 and C anything are the rare spawns. Forces exactly the reasoning the exam wants, disguised as a game.

Part G — Urban drainage

G1 · Foul and storm sewer design

PHENOMENA: READY DESIGN TABLES: NOT

The design workflow (rational method, Colebrook full-bore tables, part-full circular charts, self-cleansing checks) is catchment arithmetic and 3D geometry — paper and Butler & Davies. What the sim does, uniquely well, is the physics the design protects against: the free-surface→pressurised transition. There is a delicious meta-fact available: the solver's own equation of state IS a Preissmann slot — the identical trick InfoWorks and SWMM engines use to model surcharge. Teaching the slot with the c slider is teaching how the industry's software actually works.

Setup: template rectangular "sewer" with two manhole shafts. Vary: pipe gradient per student. Report: the inflow q at which the upstream manhole first surcharges (HGL gauge crosses soffit level). Plot: capacity vs gradient — the class rediscovers q ∝ √S, then checks it against a Manning full-bore calculation using the delivered n they measured, closing the loop between design formula and observed failure.

Exercise · design to spec — "the throttle-and-crest duel"

Spec: pass 2×DWF forward at all times, spill nothing below 3×DWF, keep chamber depth under a stated ceiling at 6×DWF. Running it in class

  • Same Resolution for everyone in any pooled exercise — delivered friction depends on Δx (limit 5). Put "Resolution: Medium" on the worksheet.
  • Wait out the spin-up countdown before reading steady values. Most scenes settle in 10–30 s; the slow ones are jet (~55 s), m2 (~90 s) and c13 (~95 s) — these are measured settle times, not padding.
  • Reading a number: the gauge chart prints its live value; the hover readout gives spot values; for clean digits, APP.probe(x, z) in the dev console, and APP.volume() for storage exercises.
  • Keep the tab visible — the loop stops when the page is hidden. The speed slider (×0.02–3) is the slow-motion control for transients.
  • Distribute set-ups as ?scene=id links; sandbox rigs travel as a recipe card (coordinates + a screenshot) until change #1 exists. Z undoes a segment, R resets the water without losing the drawing.
  • The standing rule when students change q: re-check the tailwater against yc = (q²/g)⅓ (printed on the q slider) and keep it ≥ 1.3 yc. This is a real hydraulic fact about degenerate boundary controls, so enforcing it is teaching, not admin.
